TSP heuristic lab · manual beam console

Aim the beams yourself. Move the cursor when you decide.

No algorithm runs on this page. There is a cursor, three far corners, and a beam request you can fire at any node. Ask a node for its single nearest, or its k farthest (the beam width is adjustable), and the rays stay on the map until you clear them — so you can put several answers side by side, argue with them, and only then move the cursor to the city you pick. Every move is one real edge; the tour is whatever path you walked.

How to use the console

The map is the only input. The cursor starts on the first endpoint of the farthest pair — that is corner Z's partner — and the ranked table below the map draws the pairs you pick as dashed red lines, so the pole is on the chart until you toggle it off. Click any city to make it the focus; the beam buttons always ask the focused node, so "ask any node" is just click-then-ask. Requests pile up and stay drawn across moves until you clear them or ask again — that is why the rays are dashed: they are answers, not edges. Every beam button is a toggle, so a second click also takes its answer back.

1 · ask

nearest ×1 returns the closest city to the focus — one, always, whatever the beam width says — and draws a plain ray to it. It searches the whole map, walked cities included, but leaves out the ones already wired to the focus — after A → B the nearest from B no longer names A, and the same goes for the city a jump left behind — and any city that already carries two walked edges, since nothing more can be wired into it. farthest ×k does not stop at its target: a faint ray names the k farthest, then a bright leg leaves each of them for that city's farthest city — a bounce, arrowed at both ends, so you see where the far end of the map reflects back to. The farthest list is the whole map, walked cities included, because it is the pole and should not move as you walk; the bounce only lands on cities that still have an edge free. Excluding the source keeps it a reflection rather than a retrace. rightmost ×1 builds its own axis: it fans a ray from the focus to every city, takes the middle ray by screen angle as the divider — drawn in cyan, it is the node's forward — and the half sweeping clockwise from it is the right side. The answer is the last ray of that half, the one that gets furthest clockwise before the divider comes back around behind you. Right follows the same switch: unchecked, cities already on the path stay out of the fan and can never be the answer; tick all cities to fan the whole map. zigzag chains the farthest bounce from both ends of the pole — A and Z — each hop being the farthest city not yet on that chain, drawn one hop at a time in purple for A and teal for Z. The two orders usually differ: both chains are printed under the map, one below the other, with odd stops bold. A second click hides the walks; the strings stay. Clicking the same button again removes its answer. The nearest chips carry a ↯ n badge: the number of far and corner rays that land on that city right now, so a city pulled from two directions shows up before you move.

2 · the three corners

The dashed red lines are the pairs picked in the ranked table: click a row to draw that pair, click it again to drop it, so several can sit on the map at once. Each is labelled #rank · d and ringed at both ends; rank 1 is the map's pole A–Z, drawn for you on every new map. circles adds the circle that has each drawn pair as its diameter, so every city on it sees that pair at a right angle. Z is the far end of that pole. The other two corners are the endpoints of the pair that maximises distance × |sin of the angle against the farthest pole — the longest pair that is roughly perpendicular to it. ask 3 corners makes all three report their k farthest cities at once; click it again to remove all three answers.

3 · decide, then move

Shift-click a city, click a result chip, or focus it and press move cursor → focus. That claims the edge; the beams stay where they are, so you can keep reading an old answer after moving. A move onto an unvisited city extends the leg. A move onto a city that already has one edge joins two loose ends instead: that is how a walk split by jumps gets stitched back together, and how you close the loop by hand when the cursor's loose end meets another one. Two full ends never connect, and if the final link leaves every city with two edges the tour closes itself.

3b · jump instead

Double-click a city, or press jump cursor → focus (key j), to move the cursor without drawing an edge — to a fresh city or back to one you already walked, so you can re-ask beams anywhere. The leg walked so far is sealed, a new leg starts at the jump target, and the beams stay put. A jump to a walked city branches the walk there, which Close tour then refuses until Undo takes the jumps back.

4 · close

Close tour connects the last pair of loose ends once every city is in, writing the blue closing edge. With more than two loose ends it refuses and names them, so you can link them yourself first. The gap is measured against Held–Karp (n ≤ 20) or a proven 1-tree bound.

Nothing here is automatic. The beams are queries, the cursor moves only when you say so, and the path is exactly the edges you accepted. That makes the console a place to test a rule before any rule is written.

Fine print. This page is a manual explorer, not the heuristic — src/farthest/farthest_pole.js still holds the Farthest-Beam Walk that decides by itself, but the console never calls it. A beam request is O(n) over the distance matrix — O(n log n) for the right fan's angle sort — and never mutates the path. Each button is a toggle: the second click removes exactly the answer the first click added. Nothing is drawn that tracks the cursor; the map only carries what you asked for. The cursor can only move to a city that is not already on the path, so the walked path is always simple and closing it yields a valid tour; Undo and Reset walk rebuild that state, and a jump seals the current leg instead of inventing an edge, so nothing is ever drawn that you did not accept or explicitly refuse. Distances are Euclidean on the unit square, so the numbers are map units, not kilometres. The demo caps n at 60 to keep the requests and the ranked list readable. The ranked table is a picker: click a row and that pair is drawn as a thin dashed red line, labelled #rank · d, both ends ringed; click it again to drop it, and several can stay drawn at once. Rank 1 — the farthest pair, the pole itself — is picked for you on every new map. BGMA max is the one automatic thing here: it runs the in-page heuristic at the max effort preset, draws its tour in light grey, and prints that tour under the map; clicking it again hides the ring, the string stays. The full solver in this repo, BGMA, matched the published optimum on all 9 TSPLIB instances up to n = 417 and stayed within +0.33–2.10% of it at n = 442–1060.
